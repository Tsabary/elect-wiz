## 1. haredi-enlistment: Haredi military enlistment

**Description:** Whether young Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) men who study in yeshivas should serve in the army or in civilian national service.

**More info:** For decades, full-time yeshiva students were allowed to postpone army service, which in practice meant most never served. In 2024 the Supreme Court ruled there is no legal basis for exempting them as a group. Since then the army has sent tens of thousands of call-up orders, but only a small fraction have enlisted. Laws setting gradual enlistment targets did not pass before the Knesset dissolved, and the issue is expected to be central in forming the next government.

**Question:** Should Haredi yeshiva students be required to serve?

1. Pass a law that lets full-time yeshiva students keep studying instead of serving, with no criminal or financial penalties. Torah study is a core value that has protected the Jewish people, and Haredim shouldn't be forced into a framework that threatens their way of life.
2. Bring Haredim into the army gradually: set yearly enlistment targets, create army units suited to a religious lifestyle, offer incentives, and cut yeshiva funding only if the targets aren't met. Real change will only work through agreement with Haredi leaders, without tearing society apart.
3. Require every 18-year-old, including Haredim, to serve in the army or in civilian national service, with exemptions only for a small number of outstanding Torah scholars. Those who refuse lose state benefits. The army needs the soldiers, and the burden should be shared equally.
4. No one should be forced into military or national service. Service should be voluntary, backed by strong incentives, and rights and benefits shouldn't depend on it.

## 2. cost-of-living: Cost of living

**Description:** What the state should do to lower the prices of food, products and everyday services.

**More info:** Prices in Israel are high compared with other developed countries, and surveys show the cost of living is one of the top issues for voters, Jewish and Arab alike. The debate is mainly about what works best: more competition and imports, lower taxes, the state controlling prices, or direct help for families.

**Question:** What is the best way to lower the cost of living?

1. Open the market to more competition: make it easier to import goods, accept products approved in Europe without extra Israeli requirements, cut red tape, and break up the few large companies that dominate many markets. Competition, not government control, is what brings prices down over time.
2. Cut taxes, such as VAT (the tax added to almost everything you buy) and income tax, and cut government spending and bureaucracy, leaving more money in people's pockets. People spend their own money better than the state does.
3. Have the state step in more: supervise the prices of basic food and essential services, act more strongly against big companies that overcharge, and tax unusually high profits. Without active regulation, big companies keep prices high.
4. Focus on direct help for those hit hardest: raise welfare allowances and the minimum wage, and subsidize basics such as daycare, public transport and food for low-income families. The fastest relief comes from helping households directly.

## 3. housing: Housing

**Description:** How to make buying or renting a home more affordable.

**More info:** Home prices and rents in Israel are high compared with incomes. In 2026 rents kept rising while home prices dipped slightly. In an August 2026 survey of 18-to-35-year-olds, housing prices were the top voting issue. Most land for new building belongs to the state, so government decisions strongly affect how many homes get built.

**Question:** What should the state mainly do to make housing cheaper?

1. Build much more, faster: speed up building permits, release more state-owned land for construction, and cut bureaucracy and fees. Prices are high because too few homes are built, and more homes on the market will bring prices down.
2. Have the state build long-term rental apartments at affordable prices, require every new project to include some affordable units, and regulate the rental market, including limits on how much rent can go up. The market alone has not delivered homes young people can afford.
3. Help people buy directly: discounted-price apartment programs, bigger and longer mortgages, and grants for young couples and those who served. Owning a home is the key to financial security, and the state should help people get there.
4. Make other parts of the country more attractive: invest in transport, jobs and services outside the Tel Aviv area, and renew old neighborhoods in existing cities. Prices are high because demand is crowded into the center; making other areas attractive eases the pressure.

## 4. judicial-system: Judicial system

**Description:** How power should be divided between the government, the Knesset (parliament) and the courts.

**More info:** Since 2023 the government has pushed changes to the justice system. They include how judges are chosen and the power of the attorney general, the government's top legal adviser, whose legal opinions currently bind the government. The changes led to mass protests and counter-protests. Supporters say they restore balance between the branches; opponents say they remove checks on government power. In 2024 the Supreme Court cancelled one of the changes. In 2025 the Knesset passed a law giving politicians more say in choosing judges, which takes effect when the next Knesset begins. The government also voted to fire the attorney general, a decision the Supreme Court cancelled.

**Question:** How much power should the courts have compared with the government and the Knesset?

1. Give the government and the Knesset more power over the courts: politicians get a bigger say in choosing judges, judges get less power to cancel laws or government decisions, and the attorney general's legal opinions stop binding the government. Elected representatives, not appointed officials, should have the final say.
2. Agree on clear rules through a broad compromise: write a constitution, or a basic law, that sets out exactly what each branch can and can't do, possibly letting the Knesset override a court ruling in limited cases. The rules of the game should be set by wide agreement, not by one side.
3. Reverse the recent changes, including the new law on choosing judges, and protect the independence of the courts and the attorney general in a basic law (a law with constitutional status). Independent courts are a key check on government power and protect citizens' rights.
4. Keep the system as it is today, including the changes the current government has already passed, such as the new law on choosing judges. No further changes and no reversal, with only small adjustments if needed. The country needs stability and should focus on other priorities.

## 5. october-7-inquiry: October 7 inquiry

**Description:** How the failures around the October 7 attack should be investigated.

**More info:** Under Israeli law, the government decides whether to set up a state commission of inquiry. It's the most powerful type of investigation: it can summon anyone and see any document, and its members are picked by the President of the Supreme Court. The government opposes this and proposed instead a commission picked through the Knesset, arguing that a commission picked by the Supreme Court president wouldn't be seen as impartial by much of the public. No commission of inquiry has been set up yet. The government told the Supreme Court it won't set one up before the election.

**Question:** Who should pick the people who investigate October 7?

1. The President of the Supreme Court picks the members, as the law has always required for a state commission of inquiry. Only a fully independent body with full legal powers can investigate both the government and the army, and earn public trust.
2. The Knesset picks the members, either by broad agreement or split equally between the government's supporters and the opposition, with bereaved families involved. A commission picked by the Supreme Court president wouldn't be seen as fair by much of the public.
3. A neutral method that no political side controls picks the members, for example retired judges elected by the Knesset in a secret vote. The investigation would cover the government, the army and security services, and the legal system alike. Only such a body can be trusted by both sides.

## 6. gaza: Gaza Strip policy

**Description:** What Israel should do in the Gaza Strip now that there is a ceasefire and the hostages have returned.

**More info:** A ceasefire based on a US plan has held since October 2025, and the remains of the last hostage were returned in January 2026. The plan's next steps haven't happened: disarming Hamas, bringing in an international security force, and further Israeli withdrawal. The army still controls about half of Gaza.

**Question:** What should Israel do in Gaza?

1. Go back to war and take control of all of Gaza until Hamas can no longer fight or govern, encourage Gaza residents to leave voluntarily, and allow Israeli communities to be rebuilt in Gaza. Only completely defeating Hamas will prevent another October 7.
2. Stay where the army is now and keep military and economic pressure on Hamas, with no more withdrawals or rebuilding until Hamas gives up all its weapons. Israel must keep security in its own hands and not rely on foreign forces.
3. Move forward with the US plan: an international force and a new Palestinian administration without Hamas, backed by Arab countries, while Hamas is gradually disarmed and the army gradually withdraws. A diplomatic deal will lock in the military gains and open the way to peace deals with more Arab countries.
4. End the war completely: withdraw the army from Gaza, allow aid and rebuilding without restrictions, and hand control to a Palestinian government as a step toward a Palestinian state. Continued military control won't bring security and costs lives on both sides.
5. Go back to war until Hamas is destroyed, then keep Israeli security control while a local administration without Hamas runs daily life. Israel shouldn't settle Gaza or push residents out, but it can't leave security to anyone else.

## 7. judea-samaria: Judea and Samaria

**Description:** The future of Judea and Samaria, the settlements, and relations with the Palestinians.

**More info:** Israel has not applied its law (sovereignty) to Judea and Samaria, an area also known as the West Bank. Palestinian cities there are run by the Palestinian Authority, and Israelis live in settlements. In September 2025 several Western countries, including the UK, France and Canada, recognized a Palestinian state. Over the past year the government approved thousands of new homes in settlements, and bills to apply Israeli sovereignty (also called annexation) passed early votes in the Knesset. Several countries banned goods made in settlements.

**Question:** What should Israel's long-term policy be in Judea and Samaria?

1. Make all of Judea and Samaria officially part of Israel, expand the settlements, and dismantle the Palestinian Authority. This is the historic homeland of the Jewish people, and a Palestinian state would threaten Israel's existence.
2. Make only part of the area officially Israeli: the settlements and the open areas around them (known as Area C, about 60% of the territory), or key areas such as the Jordan Valley. Palestinians would run their own daily affairs in the rest. This secures vital areas without taking in a large Palestinian population.
3. Keep things as they are: keep strengthening the settlements and keep the army free to act anywhere, without annexing territory and without a Palestinian state for now. Under current conditions, any dramatic move in either direction is too risky.
4. Two states through a negotiated agreement, where most settlers stay: the large settlement blocs near the pre-1967 border become part of Israel, Israel gives the Palestinians other land in exchange, and only the smaller, remote settlements are evacuated. This will end the conflict and keep Israel democratic with a Jewish majority.
5. Two states on the exact pre-1967 border: Israel withdraws from all of Judea and Samaria and East Jerusalem, every settlement is evacuated, and East Jerusalem becomes the Palestinian capital. Only ending Israeli control over all the land taken in 1967 can bring a just peace.
6. One state for everyone between the Jordan River and the sea, with equal rights and an equal vote for Jews and Palestinians, instead of two separate states. Separation has failed, and equality for everyone who lives in this land is the only just solution.

## 8. religion-and-state: Religion and state

**Description:** The role of religion in public life, including Shabbat, marriage, conversion and the Chief Rabbinate.

**More info:** Religious matters in Israel follow long-standing arrangements known as "the status quo". These cover marriage, conversion to Judaism, kosher certification and what's open on Shabbat, and give a central role to the Chief Rabbinate, the state's official religious authority. Marriages in Israel can only be performed by religious authorities (for Jews, the Rabbinate), though marriages performed abroad are recognized. Public transport mostly doesn't run on Shabbat, with some local exceptions. In July 2026 the Knesset also passed a basic law declaring Torah study a foundational value.

**Question:** What should the relationship between religion and state be?

1. Give Jewish religious law a bigger role in how the state runs: enforce Shabbat closures in public by law, keep marriage, divorce and conversion only through the Orthodox Rabbinate, and add more religious studies in state schools. A Jewish state should be guided by Jewish religious law, not only by tradition and culture.
2. Keep the current arrangements mostly as they are: the Rabbinate keeps control of marriage and conversion, and most public transport stays closed on Shabbat, with only small local changes. These arrangements are a compromise that lets religious and secular Israelis live together, and big changes either way would deepen the divide.
3. Keep the Rabbinate, but let people choose alternatives: civil unions for couples who don't want a religious wedding, each city decides on Shabbat public transport, and non-Orthodox or private bodies can also give kosher certificates and perform conversions. Religious services should be available, but no one should be forced to use them.
4. Take religion out of state law entirely: civil marriage and divorce for everyone, public transport and shops can open on Shabbat, and the state treats all streams of Judaism, and people with no religion, equally. Religion is a personal choice, and the state shouldn't enforce it.

## 9. crime-in-arab-society: Crime in Arab society

**Description:** How to deal with the wave of murders and organized crime in Arab towns and cities.

**More info:** 2025 was the deadliest year on record in Arab society, with 252 people killed, and the first half of 2026 was even worse. Only a small share of these murders have been solved. In July 2026 the government approved a plan to bring the Shin Bet into the fight against crime organizations. The Shin Bet is Israel's internal security service, normally focused on terrorism. The plan is funded mainly by moving about NIS 500 million from a five-year development plan for Arab communities.

**Question:** What is the right way to fight crime in Arab society?

1. Use extraordinary measures against organized crime: bring in the Shin Bet (Israel's internal security service) to investigate crime organizations in Arab communities, and give the police a new power to hold any suspected crime boss in Israel, Arab or Jewish, without trial. Ordinary policing has failed, and only powers like those used against terrorism can break these organizations.
2. Give the police far more resources, but no special powers: many more officers and investigators and a major campaign to collect illegal weapons, all under normal law. The police can solve this if they're properly staffed and equipped, with no need for the Shin Bet or detention without trial.
3. Invest in Arab communities first: restore and increase funding for education, jobs, housing and local councils so young people have alternatives to crime, working with local leaders. Crime grows out of years of neglect, and more policing alone won't fix it.

## 10. iran-and-regional-security: Iran, Hezbollah and the region

**Description:** Israel's strategy toward Iran, Hezbollah (the Iran-backed terror organization in Lebanon) and the wider region.

**More info:** Since February 2026 Israel and the United States have been fighting Iran. In March Hezbollah joined from Lebanon, leading to an Israeli ground operation in southern Lebanon. Ceasefires and US-brokered deals have been reached and partly broken. Israel has handed some areas in southern Lebanon to the Lebanese army, while fighting between the US and Iran continued into September 2026.

**Question:** What should Israel's strategy be toward Iran, Hezbollah and the region?

1. Keep attacking Iran, Hezbollah and their allies until they can no longer threaten Israel, and stay in southern Lebanon as long as needed, with no deals in the meantime. Past agreements didn't stop these threats from growing, and only force guarantees security.
2. Keep up military pressure while negotiating, and use it to reach US-backed deals, such as disarming Hezbollah and peace deals with Arab countries. Pull back only once a deal with real security guarantees is in place. Military gains need to be turned into lasting agreements alongside allies.
3. Stop attacking now and reach ceasefires on all fronts, using force only to respond if Israel is attacked. Ending the long war comes first, even before the threats are fully removed, because its human and economic cost is too heavy.
4. End all military operations and withdraw from Lebanon now, and seek a broad regional peace through diplomacy alone. Military action hasn't brought security and keeps the cycle of escalation going.
